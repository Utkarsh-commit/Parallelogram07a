Model 01
The Foundation of All Innovation

First-Principles
Thinking

The mental model that let one man cut the cost of a rocket by 10x, reduce a battery from $600/kWh to $80, and build the most valuable car company in history — by refusing to accept how things "have always been done."

PRINCIPLE 01
What It Actually Is
The Definition

First-principles thinking means breaking a problem down to its most fundamental, undeniable truths — and then reasoning upward from those truths to a new conclusion. Aristotle called them "the first basis from which a thing is known." Musk calls it "a physics way of looking at the world."

🧠 The Contrast

Most people think by analogy — "we do this because it's like what others do." First principles thinkers strip that away entirely. Musk: "The normal way is we reason by analogy. With analogy, we are doing this because it's like something else that was done." First principles asks: what do we know is actually, fundamentally true?

Foundation Model
PRINCIPLE 02
The SpaceX Battery Breakdown
Musk's Most Famous Example

EV batteries were expensive. The industry assumed: they always will be. Musk asked a different question: What is a battery actually made of? Lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite, a steel can. What do those raw materials cost on commodity markets? Approximately $80/kWh. What were manufacturers charging? $600/kWh. The gap was assembly — which is designable.

🏭 The Same Model → SpaceX

Rockets cost $65M+ per launch. Why? "Because that's what rockets cost." Musk went to the components: aluminium alloys, titanium, copper, carbon fibre. Raw materials ≈ 2% of market price. He then designed rockets in-house and made them reusable — cutting launch cost by a factor of 10.

Real-World Case
PRINCIPLE 03
📦
How Jeff Bezos Uses It
Amazon's Fundamental Truths

Bezos resisted analogical thinking at Amazon. Instead of chasing what competitors were doing, he asked: what do customers fundamentally, permanently want? His answer: lower prices, faster delivery, more selection. These don't change. He then built infrastructure (warehouses, tech, logistics) around timeless truths — not around current trends.

Bezos on Proxies

"Resist proxies. Don't mindlessly follow processes, or unquestioningly accept survey data. That is reasoning by analogy. Go out and discover fundamental truths — focus on outcomes, and real customer feedback."

Business Application
PRINCIPLE 04
The Socratic Method — How to Find First Principles
Step by Step

Step 1 — Clarify: Why do I believe this? Where did this idea come from?

Step 2 — Challenge assumptions: How do I know this is true? What if I believed the opposite?

Step 3 — Look for evidence: Can I back this up independently?

Step 4 — The test: If your "why" ends with a falsifiable fact, you've hit a first principle. If it ends with "because I said so" or "it just is" — you've hit an assumption masquerading as truth.

Your Toolkit
PRINCIPLE 05
Reasoning by Analogy vs. First Principles
The Key Distinction

Reasoning by analogy: "Electric cars are too expensive because batteries cost too much — they always have." This accepts the current state as the fundamental truth.

First principles: "What is a battery made of? What do those materials cost? What's preventing us from reaching those material costs?" This treats current state as a variable, not a constant.

⚠️ The Trap

Most industries operate almost entirely on analogical thinking. That's why most industries are blindsided when someone reasons from first principles. Airbnb, Uber, SpaceX — all emerged because someone refused to accept "the way things are."

Warning
PRINCIPLE 06
Apply It to Your Own Life
The Everyday Version

Nutrition: instead of chasing every trend, the first principles are simple: limit processed food, eat lean protein, eat vegetables. These don't go out of style. Leadership: forget TQM, Six Sigma, 5S — the first principle is: ask the people who actually do the job what they would change. Your career: instead of copying a path, ask: what skills are fundamentally valuable? What does the market actually, always reward?

The Musk Test for Volume Scalability

"If a part is still expensive when you produce a million of them per year, the reason is its design — not its manufacturing. Anything at volume can approach the cost of its raw materials." Apply this logic to any problem you face at scale.

Personal Application

"I think it's important to reason from first principles rather than by analogy. You boil things down to the most fundamental truths and then reason up from there."

— Elon Musk, Tesla & SpaceX

Model 02
Charlie Munger's Favourite Weapon

Inversion —
Ask What Fails

Instead of asking "How do I succeed?" ask "What would guarantee failure?" Then systematically avoid those things. The model that turned Charlie Munger into one of the greatest investors in history — not by being brilliant, but by being consistently not stupid.

"Invert,always invert" — Jacobi, 1800s mathematician
Rule 1Buffett: "Don't lose money." Rule 2: Don't forget Rule 1.
Betterto avoid stupidity than to seek brilliance — Munger
Pre-mortem: assume failure before starting, then reverse-engineer why
INVERSION 01
What Inversion Actually Is
The Flip

Inversion means turning a problem upside down. Instead of asking "How do I achieve X?" you ask "What would guarantee I never achieve X?" Instead of "How do I build a great team?" you ask "What would destroy a great team?" Then you avoid those things with ruthless discipline. As Munger says: "Invert, always invert."

🔬 The Psychological Reason It Works

Our brains are better at spotting potential negatives than envisioning an ideal path. Inversion works with your brain's natural wiring, not against it. Loss aversion, the same bias that kills investors, becomes an asset when used deliberately through inversion.

Core Concept
INVERSION 02
How Munger & Buffett Use It
Berkshire's Secret

When evaluating an investment, Munger doesn't ask: "How can this stock rise?" He asks: "What would cause this company to decline?" — identifying excessive debt, poor management, shrinking moats. Buffett's two rules of investing are pure inversion: Rule 1: Don't lose money. Rule 2: Don't forget Rule 1. If you can just not lose, the gains take care of themselves.

Munger's Best Line on This

"It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent." Avoiding stupidity is often more valuable than seeking genius.

Investing Application
INVERSION 03
💀
The Pre-Mortem
Before You Launch Anything

Before starting a project, imagine it is 6 months in the future and your project has completely failed. Ask: "Why did it fail?" Write down every reason. Then eliminate every single one of those failure modes before you begin. This is Gary Klein's pre-mortem — inversion applied to project planning — and it consistently surfaces risks that forward planning completely misses.

🌊 Real Example — CIA Counterterrorism

After 9/11, Phillip Mudd of the CIA reframed everything through inversion. Instead of asking "Where is bin Laden?" he asked "How do we protect Americans?" This inverted question unlocked an entirely different strategy — and a far more effective one than the original forward framing.

Power Move
INVERSION 04
The Johnny Carson Speech
Munger's Favourite Example

When asked to give a commencement speech on "How to Be Happy," comedian Johnny Carson inverted the entire question and listed exactly how to be miserable instead. Be unreliable. Learn only from your own experience. Stay down when life knocks you down. Wallow in self-pity. Avoid the opposite of those things — and success follows naturally. Munger cited this speech repeatedly as one of the most elegant demonstrations of inversion in life.

Life Application
INVERSION 05
Inversion in Practice: 5 Templates
Apply It Now

Want better collaboration? Ask: "How would we completely destroy team collaboration?" Then prevent that.

Want to increase sales? Ask: "How could we drive sales as low as possible, as fast as possible?" Then eliminate those forces.

Want a great product launch? Ask: "What would make this launch a complete disaster?" Then build safeguards.

Want a healthy life? Ask: "What habits would guarantee I destroy my health?" Stop doing those things.

Want great relationships? Ask: "What would make me impossible to be around?" Then become the opposite.

Templates
INVERSION 06
The Cognitive Obstacle: Inconsistency Avoidance
Why Inversion Is Hard

The moment you make a decision, you love it more than you did a second before. You don't want to be inconsistent in your beliefs — this is what Munger calls "Inconsistency Avoidance Tendency." When you try to invert, your brain resists destroying its own favorite ideas. Munger's response: "Any year that you don't destroy one of your best-loved ideas is probably a wasted year." The antidote is to schedule inversion deliberately — not just when it feels comfortable.

🔬 Also: Sunk Cost Bias

Once you've predicted the rewards of a decision and committed, you're incentivized to believe the scenario will play out. To invert properly, you must mentally "give up" on those rewards before starting — which is why writing kill criteria before starting any project changes everything.

Cognitive Trap
🚨 Munger's Single Most Important Quote on Inversion

"All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." — This encapsulates the entire model in one sentence. Don't optimize for arriving at success. Identify the roads that lead to failure. Then never take them.

"It is not enough to think problems through forward. You must also think in reverse. Invert, always invert. Many hard problems can only be solved when addressed backward."

— Charlie Munger, Poor Charlie's Almanack

Model 03
The Meta-Principle of All Decision Making

The Map Is
Not The Territory

Your mental model of the world is not the world itself. Every belief, framework, and assumption you carry is a simplified map — and outdated, incomplete maps are the single biggest source of bad decisions in business and life.

What "The Map" Is

The map is any mental model, belief, framework, policy, financial report, or assumption you're using to navigate reality. Maps are essential — you cannot explore every inch of territory for yourself. But every map has three fundamental limitations: Omission (it leaves things out), Distortion (it changes proportions), and Time-boundedness (it represents a snapshot that may no longer exist). The trap: mistaking the map for the territory itself.

What "The Territory" Is

The territory is reality — complex, messy, ever-shifting, and far richer than any map of it. Alfred Korzybski, who coined the phrase, warned that language and models are like "blurry glasses" — useful for filtering complexity, but dangerous when we forget they filter. Jeff Bezos proved this empirically: he once discovered that Amazon's call centre wait times were being misreported — by calling the number himself and waiting on hold for 10 minutes. He touched the territory. The map was wrong.

MAP LESSON 01
📰
Where Maps Most Often Mislead
The Usual Suspects

Financial statements: Distill thousands of transactions into something legible — but tell you nothing about employee morale, product quality, or what's really happening inside the business.

Résumés: A map of experience and education — not the person. Many great hires are ignored because their map doesn't look right.

Social media profiles: A curated, manufactured map designed to project a specific image — not a window into actual life.

Business plans: A map of a hoped-for future — the market doesn't read them.

Common Traps
MAP LESSON 02
Maps Go Out of Date
The Most Dangerous Kind of Wrong

A 16th-century map of Spain is clearly wrong — no one would use it to drive today. But people use 16th-century mental models to make decisions every day, and never notice. Your beliefs about your market, your competitors, your customers, your own skills — when were they last updated? Nobody would travel with a 400-year-old map. But millions of people make decisions based on assumptions formed 10 years ago without questioning them.

🔬 Model Bias

We become so attached to our maps that we ignore contradictory evidence from the territory. Psychologists call this confirmation bias. Entrepreneurs call it their business model. One common pitfall: Becoming attached to your map of a customer segment that stopped existing two years ago.

Hidden Risk
MAP LESSON 03
The Reification Trap
When the Map Becomes Real

Reification is when you start treating the map as if it were the territory. Examples: a spreadsheet model "coming to life" — where the model creates its own reality and everyone stops questioning whether the numbers reflect what's actually happening. Org charts that create departmental silos where actual work crosses boundaries. Metrics that everyone hits while the underlying reality they measure deteriorates.

🏙️ Urban Planning Example

City planners in Helsinki studied "desire paths" — where people actually walked versus where official paths were placed. They redesigned parks around observed reality, not the original map. Teams estimating probabilities rather than treating maps as fixed facts reduced bad decisions by 41% in a 2023 study.

Advanced Trap
MAP LESSON 04
How to Update Your Maps
The Practice

Ask before every major decision: "What assumptions am I making? Are they still valid?"

Seek disconfirming evidence: Actively look for information that challenges your beliefs. Darwin paid special attention to evidence that contradicted his own theories.

Use multiple models: No single map captures all of reality. Use a latticework of mental models to challenge each other. All models are wrong — but some combination gets closer to the truth.

Update apps regularly: Think of your mental models like software — schedule regular updates, not just when you feel the map is wrong.

Your Practice
MAP LESSON 05
The Mercator Projection Problem
Distortion at Scale

The Mercator projection map makes Greenland appear the same size as Africa. In reality, Africa is 14 times larger. This isn't a trivial cartographic quirk — it's a perfect metaphor for how our mental models systematically distort the sizes and importances of things we perceive. Cognitive biases are not random. They have consistent, predictable distortion patterns — which means you can learn to correct for them, like adjusting a map for a known projection error.

Concept
MAP LESSON 06
Choose Your Cartographers Wisely
When You Can't Make the Map Yourself

In many areas of life, you rely on maps made by others: experts, teachers, media, mentors. You cannot always go into the territory yourself. Shane Parrish: "The best we can do is choose our mapmakers wisely — and seek those who are rigorous, transparent, and open to revision." A map-maker who has never updated their map, who refuses to show their methodology, and who claims perfect accuracy — is the most dangerous kind.

Practical Wisdom

"We run into problems when our knowledge becomes knowledge of the map rather than of the actual underlying territory it describes. Reality is messy and complicated — our tendency to simplify it is understandable. But if the aim becomes simplification rather than understanding, we start to make bad decisions."

— Shane Parrish, The Great Mental Models

Model 04
The Edge That Separates Good from Great

Second-Order
Thinking

First-order thinking asks: "What happens next?" Second-order thinking asks: "And then what?" The world is full of decisions that looked brilliant on first-order analysis and created disasters through second-order effects. The 5% of people who think in second order consistently outperform everyone else.

🐢 First-Order Thinking
I'm hungry → eat a chocolate bar. Fast. Easy. No deeper thinking.
"The stock looks good → buy it." Surface-level. What everyone already sees.
Suppress small forest fires. First order: prevent local damage. Done.
Give team an ambitious production target. First order: more productivity. Win.
Prescribe antibiotics for every infection. First order: cure the infection. Done.
Introduce a cane toad to Australia to kill pests. First order: solve agricultural problem.
🦅 Second-Order Thinking
I'm hungry → why? What does my body actually need? What's the second-order effect of sugar right now?
"Everyone thinks it's good, so it's priced in. What happens if earnings miss by 3%?" → Actually sell.
Decades of suppressed fires → accumulated dry undergrowth → catastrophic inferno that destroys entire forests.
Team hits 130% → warehouse can't handle influx → demand drops → costs spiral. Net result: negative.
Overuse of antibiotics → resistant bacteria strains → infections that cannot be treated → a future medical crisis.
Cane toads are poisonous to local predators → they multiply uncontrolled → they become an invasive plague for decades.
2ND ORDER 01
Howard Marks' Framework
The Most Important Thing

Howard Marks coined the term "second-level thinking" in his book The Most Important Thing. He wrote: "First-level thinking is simplistic and superficial, and just about everyone can do it. Second-level thinking is deep, complex and convoluted." The key insight: with first-level thinking, everyone reaches the same answer. With second-level thinking, you see what others cannot.

🔬 The Investing Edge

First-level: "It's a good company; buy the stock." Second-level: "It's a good company, but everyone thinks it's a great company — and it's not. So the stock is overpriced; sell." First-level: "Bad earnings outlook; sell." Second-level: "Earnings will fall less than the market expects, and the relief will lift the stock; buy." The edge is always in seeing deeper than the consensus.

Investing Application
2ND ORDER 02
🕳️
Why People Default to First Order
The Hard Truth

The brain is wired to perform first-order thinking intuitively — it's fast, low-energy, and usually sufficient for basic survival. Second-order thinking is deliberate, slow, and energy-intensive. It requires asking "and then what?" repeatedly until you reach a natural stopping point. Howard Marks: "The difference in workload between first-level and second-level thinking is clearly massive, and the number of people capable of the latter is tiny compared to the number capable of the former."

Cognitive Reality
2ND ORDER 03
The 10-10-10 Questions
The Tool

Before any major decision, ask:

In 10 minutes, how will I feel about this decision?

In 10 months, how will I feel? What consequences will have emerged?

In 10 years, how will I feel looking back? What compounding effects — positive or negative — will have accumulated?

A decision that looks great in 10 minutes but terrible in 10 years is a first-order decision disguised as a good one.

Your Toolkit
2ND ORDER 04
🦋
Unintended Consequences Are Inevitable
The Honest Reckoning

The research is clear: over 83% of business failures can be traced to ignoring second-order and indirect consequences. Cutting employee training saves money in Q1 — but creates skill gaps that cost 10× that savings in Year 2. Offering unlimited PTO sounds generous — but without clear norms, nobody takes it, guilt compounds, and burnout rises. Amazon's early strategy of investing in infrastructure at a loss looked terrible in first order — and created an insurmountable moat in second order.

Warning
✅ How to Build This Habit

After every decision, before executing: write down first-order consequences. Then write second-order consequences. Then third, if relevant. Ask: "What behaviors will this incentivize?" "How will people adapt?" "What feedback loops does this create?" Ask "why" at least 5 times to trace the consequence chain. Start small — apply it to one decision per day. It compounds.

"Every action has consequences, and those consequences have consequences. The world is full of decisions that looked good based on first-order thinking but created disasters through second-order effects."

— Howard Marks, The Most Important Thing

Model 05
Warren Buffett's Most Underrated Edge

Circle of
Competence

Knowing what you know — and what you don't — is more valuable than knowing everything. The circle of competence isn't about being the smartest person in the room. It's about knowing which room you belong in.

CIRCLE 01
⭕
The Core Idea

Every person has a domain of genuine expertise — a circle. Inside the circle, your knowledge is deep, your judgment is reliable, and your decisions are sound. Outside the circle, you're navigating without a map, at the mercy of currents and storms you don't fully understand. Buffett: "You don't have to be an expert on every company. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important. Knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."

Core Concept
CIRCLE 02
The Ego Problem

When ego drives what you undertake — rather than competence — you develop blind spots. Munger: "Rise quite high in life by slowly developing a circle of competence — which results partly from what you were born with and partly from what you slowly develop through work." The failure mode: overestimating the radius of your circle. The trap of "I understand businesses, therefore I can invest in any business." Apple and a fruit vendor are both "businesses."

🔬 Dunning-Kruger Effect

The people with the least knowledge in a domain consistently overestimate their competence in it. The most dangerous investors, leaders, and decision-makers are those who don't know what they don't know — and never will unless they're actively looking for the boundary.

The Trap
CIRCLE 03
Expanding the Circle

Knowing your circle's limits is not a constraint — it's a competitive advantage. And it is not static. You can expand your circle deliberately, over time, through deep study. Charlie Munger built a latticework of circles — mental models drawn from mathematics, philosophy, biology, economics, and psychology. The goal is not to become an expert in everything, but to build enough adjacent knowledge that you can recognize when a problem belongs inside your circle — and when it doesn't.

Model 06
The Simplicity Principle

The Razors —
Occam & Hanlon

Two of the most useful thinking tools in existence. One helps you cut through complexity. The other helps you preserve relationships and reduce paranoia. Both save enormous amounts of wasted mental energy.

🪒
Occam's Razor

When faced with competing explanations or hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions is most likely correct. Named after 14th-century philosopher William of Ockham. Einstein used it in special relativity. Darwin used it to explain natural selection. The rule: don't multiply complexity beyond necessity.

In practice: When something breaks, your first instinct is often to build an elaborate theory. Occam's Razor says: what's the simplest explanation that fits all the facts? Start there. Most of the time, you'll find it.

The limit: Sometimes reality is genuinely complex. "Everything should be made as simple as possible — but no simpler." — Einstein. Don't oversimplify to the point of losing explanatory power.

🧹
Hanlon's Razor

"Never attribute to malice that which can be adequately explained by stupidity." In other words: when something bad happens to you, the most likely explanation is error or incompetence — not conspiracy or deliberate hostility.

Why it matters: Humans are wired to see threats and hostile intent everywhere. This made sense on the savanna. It makes terrible sense in modern organisations. Most frustrating things that happen to you are caused by people who: forgot, made a mistake, had incomplete information, or were under pressure.

The power: When you assume stupidity instead of malice, you respond with empathy instead of defensiveness. You find practical solutions instead of enemies. You reduce drama by roughly 90%.

RAZOR NOTE 01
Hanlon's Razor in Daily Life

Colleague didn't respond to your email → probably busy or it got lost, not ignoring you deliberately. Manager gave you critical feedback → probably trying to help, not attacking you. Partner snapped at you → probably stressed, not hostile. Supplier delivered late → probably had a real problem, not sabotaging you. This model doesn't mean everyone is innocent. It means start with the charitable explanation — then adjust only when evidence demands otherwise.

Relationship Tool
RAZOR NOTE 02
How They Work Together

Occam's Razor and Hanlon's Razor are deeply connected. Hanlon's is essentially Occam's applied specifically to human behavior. When someone does something bad, Occam says: "what's the simplest explanation?" Hanlon says: "incompetence requires fewer assumptions than malice." Used together, they form a powerful shield against paranoia, conspiracy thinking, and unnecessary conflict — three of the biggest drains on productive thinking.

Combined Power
RAZOR NOTE 03
Occam's Razor & Napoleon's Wisdom

Napoleon reportedly said: "Never ascribe to malice that which is adequately explained by incompetence." This predates Hanlon and shows how ancient this wisdom is. The oldest version appears in Robert Heinlein's 1941 novella Logic of Empire: "You have attributed conditions to villainy that simply result from stupidity." The enduring lesson: our evolutionary threat-detection systems consistently overdiagnose hostile intent. These razors are the correction.

Model 07
Thinking in Odds, Not Absolutes

Probabilistic
Thinking

Most people think in binary: this will work, or it won't. The best thinkers in the world think in distributions: "There's a 70% chance of X, a 20% chance of Y, and a 10% chance of Z." This single shift produces dramatically better decisions.

1
Identify What Actually Matters

Not all variables are equal. Probabilistic thinking starts with identifying the 2–3 variables that most heavily determine the outcome. Most people overweight noise and underweight signal. What are the key drivers? Focus on those.

2
Estimate the Odds Honestly

Assign actual probabilities — not "likely" or "unlikely." "I think there's a 65% chance this customer closes." This sounds uncomfortable because it forces honesty. That discomfort is the point. Fuzzy language hides fuzzy thinking.

3
Check Your Assumptions

Where did your probability estimate come from? What's your base rate? (How often does this type of thing succeed in general?) What biases might be inflating or deflating your estimate? Compare your view to the consensus — and know why you're different, if you are.

4
Decide — and Then Update

Probabilistic thinkers update their beliefs continuously as new information arrives. What seemed 70% likely yesterday may be 40% likely today. This is what distinguishes great forecasters from everyone else — not that they're always right, but that they update efficiently. Clinging to a view in the face of new evidence is what kills decisions, not the original estimate.

✅ Jeff Bezos: The Regret Minimization Framework

A bonus model from Bezos — and perhaps the most emotionally intelligent mental model in this guide. Whenever facing a big decision, project yourself to age 80, looking back. Ask: "Will I regret not doing this?" Most of the things we most fear in the short term become trivially unimportant from that vantage point. Most of the things we regret most are not the things we tried and failed — they are the things we were too afraid to try. Bezos used this framework to leave his Wall Street career and start Amazon. He knew he wouldn't regret trying. He knew he would regret not trying.
