Chapter 01
Grillner 2025 · PNAS · Dorsolateral Striatum · Basal Ganglia

The Dopamine
Loop Explained

Most people try to quit a habit by fighting the behaviour directly — through willpower, motivation, and sheer determination. This almost always fails. Not because they lack character. Because they're fighting the wrong thing. The behaviour isn't the problem. The neural loop that triggers it is. Here's what's actually happening in your brain — and why that changes everything.

DLSdorsolateral striatum — where habits are stored after becoming automatic (Grillner 2025)
Autoonce a habit consolidates in the basal ganglia — the cortex is no longer needed
Cueby the time you consciously register the behaviour — it has already started
Loopevery habit is: Cue → Routine → Reward. All 3 must be addressed to break it.
C
Part 1 of 3
The Cue

A sensory or contextual trigger that fires the habit circuit — automatically, without your conscious decision. Cues can be: a time of day (5PM → pour a drink), a location (couch → reach for the phone), an emotional state (stress → snack), a person (certain friend → smoke), or a sequence that immediately precedes the habit (sit down → light up). By the time you consciously register the craving — the cue already fired the loop 300–500 milliseconds ago.

Break it here: Identify every cue that fires your specific habit. Map them all. You cannot dismantle a trigger you haven't named. The cue list IS the quitting strategy.
R
Part 2 of 3
The Routine

The automatic behaviour itself — stored in the dorsolateral striatum (DLS) as a sequence that runs independently of conscious decision-making. Research published in PNAS (Grillner, 2025) isolated the DLS as the specific structure where habit sequences are encoded. Once a habit is consolidated here, it runs even when you're thinking about something completely different. You can be fully resolved not to do it — and your hands are already doing it. This is not weakness. It is neurology.

Break it here: You cannot delete a habit routine — only replace it. The neural groove doesn't disappear. You build a parallel groove through a different behaviour that earns the same reward.
R
Part 3 of 3
The Reward

The neurochemical payoff that reinforces the loop. Rewards can be neurochemical (dopamine, stress relief, temporary pleasure), emotional (distraction from discomfort, sense of control), or social (belonging, acceptance, identity reinforcement). The critical insight: the perceived reward is what matters — not the actual outcome. The cigarette feels stress-relieving even if it's physiologically stress-producing. The loop doesn't care about accuracy. It cares about reinforcement.

Break it here: Find the reward your habit is actually providing. Not what you think it is. What it actually is. You need to provide that reward through a different mechanism.
DOPAMINE SCIENCE 01
Dopamine Is Not Pleasure — It Is Anticipation

The most important and most misunderstood fact about dopamine: it is not the pleasure chemical. It is the anticipation chemical. Dopamine spikes before the reward — in the moment of craving and anticipation. After the reward, dopamine actually drops below baseline. This is why habits feel compelling before you engage in them and hollow or shameful after. The loop is engineered to keep you seeking — not satisfied. Understanding this changes your relationship to cravings: the craving is dopamine anticipation, not evidence that you need the thing. It will pass without the behaviour — because the behaviour doesn't actually satisfy it.

🔬 The Practical Implication

When a craving peaks — you are at the moment of maximum dopamine anticipation, not maximum need. If you wait 10–15 minutes without acting, the dopamine spike subsides and the craving physically diminishes. This is why "urge surfing" works: the wave is real. It also crests and subsides on its own if you don't act on it.

Core Science
DOPAMINE SCIENCE 02
Why Willpower Doesn't Work

Willpower operates in the prefrontal cortex — the conscious, rational, planning brain. Bad habits operate in the dorsolateral striatum — the automatic, unconscious, pattern-executing system. These are different neural systems, operating on different timescales, with different priority levels. When the basal ganglia fires a habit sequence, the prefrontal cortex doesn't even know it's happening until the behaviour has already started. You're being outmanoeuvred by your own nervous system before you have a chance to exercise willpower. The solution: change the conditions, not the determination.

Critical Truth
DOPAMINE SCIENCE 03
Urge Surfing — The Mindful Alternative

Developed by addiction researcher Alan Marlatt, urge surfing is the practice of observing a craving without acting on it — treating it like a wave that rises, peaks, and falls without requiring you to ride it. The technique: when a craving hits, label it ("I notice an urge to [X]"). Describe its physical sensations without judgment. Watch it with curiosity rather than alarm. Research shows urge surfing significantly reduces acting on cravings — because it interrupts the automatic nature of the cue-routine firing by inserting conscious observation between them. The 10–20 minutes of discomfort always passes. What you do during those minutes determines whether the habit survives.

🌿 The Research

Bowen & Marlatt (2009): urge surfing significantly reduced smoking relapse compared to control groups. The mechanism: mindful awareness of a craving as a passing mental event (not a command) breaks the automaticity that gives the loop its power.

Best Tool

"By the time you consciously register you're about to reach for your phone, your basal ganglia already fired the routine. The battle isn't against the behaviour — it's against the trigger that fired before you noticed."

— Grillner 2025 · PNAS · Habit Storage in the Dorsolateral Striatum

Chapter 02
James Clear · BJ Fogg · Reverse Habit Stacking · 2025

Habit Stacking
in Reverse

James Clear's habit stacking says: attach a new good habit to an existing trigger. Reverse habit stacking says: identify every trigger for the bad habit and systematically dismantle it — before it activates the craving. You cannot quit a habit you don't map first. Most people try. They fail. Because they're fighting the fire without removing the fuel.

5categories of triggers: Time, Location, Emotional State, People, Preceding Events
Mapthe triggers first — you cannot dismantle what you haven't named
Beforeinterrupt the trigger before it fires the craving — not after
Auditthe trigger audit is the most powerful single day-one action for any habit
🕐
Trigger Type 01
Time Triggers

A specific time of day that automatically fires the routine. 5PM → drink. After dinner → scroll. Midnight → snack. The brain associates clock-time with the reward so strongly that the craving begins before you've consciously registered the time.

Dismantle: Change your 5PM activity completely — a walk, a workout, a call — before the time-craving association fires. Eliminate the open slot that the habit fills.
Trigger Type 02
Location Triggers

Specific places that fire the habit automatically. The couch → TV binge. The car → fast food. The work bathroom → vape. The bar → cigarette. Location is one of the most powerful habit cues because it carries the entire associated context.

Dismantle: Change the location entirely, or change what you do immediately upon entering it. Couch = book, not remote. Car = podcast, not drive-through route.
😤
Trigger Type 03
Emotional State Triggers

Stress → cigarette. Boredom → scroll. Anxiety → drink. Loneliness → online shopping. The habit provides emotional regulation — and the brain has wired it as the go-to coping response. This is the deepest trigger to address.

Dismantle: Name the emotion when it arrives. Then use an alternative regulation tool (exercise, breathing, a phone call) before the habit fires. Emotion awareness precedes habit interruption.
Trigger Type 04
People Triggers

Specific people whose presence automatically activates the habit — smoking friends, drinking colleagues, people who enable a pattern. Social contagion is real: we unconsciously adopt the behaviours of those around us as a belonging signal.

Dismantle: Change the context of meetings with these people (coffee, not bar), be explicit about your change, or temporarily reduce contact while building the new neural pattern.
Trigger Type 05
Preceding Events

A specific sequence that always precedes the habit. Finish dinner → sweet. Open laptop → social media. Lock the car → check phone. The preceding event is so consistently followed by the habit that the brain treats them as one unit.

Dismantle: Insert a new behaviour between the preceding event and the old habit. Break the sequence. Finish dinner → clean the kitchen (15 min gap that interrupts the automatic connection).
The Trigger Audit
Map Every Trigger

Your most powerful Day 1 action: spend 20 minutes mapping every trigger for your habit across all 5 categories. Write them all down. This single exercise reveals the full architecture of the habit — and transforms a vague intention to quit into a specific, structural plan for dismantling it trigger by trigger.

Tool: For each trigger: What fires it? When does it fire? What can I do differently at that exact moment? One page. Twenty minutes. More powerful than a month of willpower.

"You don't rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems. Every trigger is a flaw in your system — not a flaw in your character."

— James Clear · Atomic Habits · Based on Charles Duhigg's Habit Loop Research

Chapter 03
Neurochemistry · Withdrawal Science · Hour-by-Hour Protocol

The 72-Hour
Rule

The first 72 hours of quitting any habitual behaviour are neurochemically the hardest — because your brain is actively recalibrating its reward system. Understanding what is happening hour by hour transforms the experience from terrifying uncertainty to predictable, survivable biology. This is what your body and brain are doing — and what to do about it.

72hrsmost critical window — dopamine system is actively recalibrating
Peakcravings peak at 24–48 hours for most behavioural habits — then begin declining
Biologythe discomfort is neurochemical recalibration — not evidence you can't do this
Ifyou survive 72 hours — the neurochemical storm is passing and the path gets clearer
Hour 0–6
The Decision
🚀 Decision Fatigue Is Coming — Prepare Now

In the first hours, motivation is high. This is the most dangerous phase because it creates overconfidence. Don't rely on willpower — set up your environment now, while you have clarity. Remove the substance or device that enables the habit. Tell at least one person. Activate your replacement behaviour immediately. Don't wait until the craving hits to figure out what you'll do instead.

→ Action: Write down your reasons for quitting. Read them when the dopamine storm hits. You won't be able to think clearly then — write it clearly now.

Hour 6–24
First Wave
🌊 The First Craving Wave — It Feels Bigger Than It Is

The dopamine system begins its loudest protest. Cravings intensify because the brain is expecting its usual reinforcement and not receiving it. This produces restlessness, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and an overwhelming sense that the habit is the only thing that will help. This feeling is completely neurochemical — not evidence that you need the thing. It is evidence that the loop was deeply wired. That's all. Urge surf through it. Each wave you survive weakens the next one.

→ Action: Use urge surfing. Set a timer for 15 minutes when a craving hits. Do something physical. The wave will pass before the timer ends.

Hour 24–48
Peak Storm
⚡ The 24-48 Hour Peak — The Hardest Window

For most behavioural habits, cravings peak between 24 and 48 hours. Sleep may be disrupted. Mood may be low. The brain's reward system is operating at deficit — dopamine baseline has dropped because the usual source has been removed and the new system hasn't yet kicked in. This is temporary. Every person who has successfully quit anything has passed through this exact window. It is not a sign that you cannot do this. It is a sign that you are doing it.

→ Action: Plan this window in advance. Fill it with high-engagement activities. Don't spend it alone, bored, with access to the habit. Remove yourself from the trigger environment entirely.

Hour 48–72
The Turn
🌅 Hour 48–72: The Neurochemical Turn Begins

By 48–72 hours, for most behavioural habits, the acute neurochemical storm begins to subside. Dopamine is recalibrating toward baseline without the external stimulus. Clarity improves. Sleep begins normalising. The emotional case for quitting starts becoming more accessible than the craving case. This doesn't mean the work is done — it means the critical survival phase is passing. The psychological battle shifts from "can I survive this?" to "can I maintain this?" — which is a fundamentally different and more manageable question.

→ Action: Acknowledge the 72-hour mark deliberately. It is a real neurobiological milestone. Celebrate it — not with the old habit, with something that genuinely rewards the achievement.

Day 4–21
New Path
🌿 Days 4–21: Building the New Neural Pathway

After 72 hours, the acute phase is over. Now the longer work begins: consistently choosing the replacement behaviour and allowing the new neural path to strengthen through repetition. Cravings will still occur — especially when old triggers fire. But they will be shorter, less intense, and less compelling each time you don't act on them. Each time you choose differently, you are literally reshaping the architecture of your brain. This is neuroplasticity in action — not a metaphor, but measurable structural change in neural connectivity.

→ Action: Track every day with a simple mark. The chain becomes a source of identity and motivation. Every mark says: "I am becoming someone who doesn't do this."

✅ The 72-Hour Emergency Kit — Prepare It Before Day 1

Before your quit date, prepare: Written list of your reasons. Read it when the storm hits. Three replacement activities for when cravings peak (exercise, call a friend, cold shower — all neurochemically interrupt the craving cycle). One person on standby who knows you're quitting and will pick up the phone. Your environment cleared of all objects, substances, or devices that enable the habit. The 72-hour window is survivable with preparation. It is very difficult without it.

Chapter 04
James Clear · Atomic Habits · Identity-Based Change

Building
Identity

Every sustainable behaviour change at its deepest level is an identity change. Most people try to quit habits from the outside in: "I'm trying to stop smoking." The most powerful shift is from the inside out: "I am a non-smoker." The behaviour follows the identity — not the reverse. Here is how to build the identity before you've earned all the evidence for it.

Outcome-Based Quitting (Why It Fails)

"I want to stop scrolling social media."
"I'm trying to quit smoking."
"I'm going to cut back on alcohol."
"I need to stop eating junk food."

All of these frame the identity as someone who has a bad habit they're fighting. The internal story is: "I am a person who does this — I'm trying not to." Every craving, every moment of weakness, confirms the original identity. The behaviour and the identity reinforce each other in the wrong direction.

Outcome-based quitting relies entirely on motivation — which is finite, variable, and exhausted by the neurochemical storm of the first 72 hours.

Identity-Based Quitting (Why It Works)

"I am a non-smoker."
"I am someone who doesn't drink."
"I am a person who protects their sleep."
"I am becoming someone who runs."

The identity is declared first. The behaviour follows as evidence of the identity — not as a struggle against the old one. Every time you choose differently, you cast a vote for the new identity. The behaviour and the identity now reinforce each other in the right direction.

James Clear: "Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become." You don't need to win unanimously. You just need the majority of votes to shift.

The Identity Ladder — From "I'm Trying to Quit" to "This Is Who I Am"

1
"I'm going to try to cut back."

The weakest identity position. Implies the old identity is still dominant. "Trying" is hope without commitment. Every relapse confirms "trying" doesn't work — which becomes the story.

2
"I'm in the process of quitting [habit]."

Stronger. Acknowledges the journey without the old identity. The word "process" creates psychological permission for imperfection while maintaining direction. Respectable starting identity statement.

3
"I don't do [habit]. It's not something I do."

Significantly more powerful. "I don't" is categorically different from "I can't." "I can't" implies external restriction. "I don't" implies internal identity. Research shows "I don't" language reduces cravings and improves quit rates vs "I can't."

4
"I am someone who prioritises [value the habit was violating]."

Moves from "not X" to "actively Y." "I am someone who protects their health/sleep/clarity/finances." This frames the identity in terms of what you're moving toward — not what you're leaving. The pull forward is more sustainable than the push away.

5
"This is simply who I am. I don't relate to that option."

The final identity state. The old habit has been so thoroughly integrated out of the identity that it no longer feels like temptation or effort — just an option that doesn't apply to you. This is the goal. It takes months to reach. Every vote builds it.

Chapter 05
Charles Duhigg · The Power of Habit · Replacement Not Removal

The Replacement
Protocol

The single most important practical insight in all of habit science: you cannot simply stop a habit. The neural loop does not disappear when you stop feeding it. It lies dormant — waiting for the cue to fire again. The only reliable method is replacement: keeping the cue and the reward, changing the routine. This is why cold turkey often fails — and strategic replacement often succeeds.

REPLACEMENT RULE 01
The Golden Rule of Habit Change

Charles Duhigg's "Golden Rule": you can never truly extinguish a bad habit — you can only change it. The cue and the reward stay the same. The routine changes. This is why Alcoholics Anonymous works: it keeps the same social ritual (meetings, community, conversation) — but replaces the substance with connection. The brain gets its social reward through a different mechanism. The loop is restructured, not deleted. Identify the reward your habit is providing, then find a behaviour that provides the same reward through a healthier mechanism.

🔬 The Evidence

Studies on smoking cessation consistently show that replacement behaviours (exercise, nicotine replacement, mindfulness) produce significantly higher quit rates than pure abstinence strategies. The brain needs an alternative route to the reward — not just a blocked road.

Core Rule
REPLACEMENT RULE 02
Match the Replacement to the Real Reward

The replacement behaviour must provide the same core reward as the old one — or the brain will reject it and route back to the original. Diagnostic questions: What does this habit give me? Stress relief → try exercise, breathing, cold water. Social belonging → try a group activity, a call. Distraction from boredom → try a stimulating book, a project. Pleasure/sensation → try a strong sensory experience (coffee, dark chocolate, spicy food). The match must be honest, not optimistic. "I'll just meditate instead of smoking" fails if meditation doesn't actually meet the stress-relief need in the moment.

Diagnostic Tool
REPLACEMENT RULE 03
Make the Replacement Immediately Rewarding

The replacement behaviour must be immediately rewarding — not just good for you in the future. The brain's habit system runs on immediate feedback. Exercise is good for you in 6 weeks — but if there's no immediate reward, it won't compete with the instant gratification of the old habit in the craving moment. Solutions: exercise with music you love, call a friend you genuinely enjoy, make the replacement genuinely pleasurable in the moment. The long-term benefits are irrelevant to the dopamine system that runs habits. Immediate reward is the only currency that matters during the transition.

Non-Negotiable
REPLACEMENT EXAMPLES
Replacement Pairings That Work
Same Reward, Different Route

Smoking → Stress relief: Ice cold glass of water, box breathing (4-4-4-4), 10-minute walk outside.

Social media → Distraction/novelty: A podcast, a book, a genuinely interesting game or puzzle.

Alcohol → Social belonging/relaxation: Non-alcoholic rituals (sparkling water, a specific glass), social settings without drinking cues.

Late-night snacking → Comfort/oral stimulation: Herbal tea, dark chocolate (1–2 pieces), a specific ritual that signals "day is done."

Doom-scrolling → Boredom escape: Skill-based games, journaling, reading, anything requiring active engagement.

Applied
REPLACEMENT SCIENCE
How Long the Replacement Takes

Phillippa Lally's UCL research (2010) found the average time to automate a new habit is 66 days — with a range of 18 to 254 days depending on complexity and individual differences. The popular "21-day habit" claim significantly underestimates the real timeline. Practically: the replacement behaviour will feel deliberate and effortful for the first 3–6 weeks. Between 6–10 weeks, it begins feeling more natural. By week 10–12 for simple behaviours, it becomes the default. Give it the actual time it requires — not the time you wish it required.

Realistic Timeline
REPLACEMENT ADVANCED
The Opposite Habit Strategy

For the most entrenched habits, consider building the opposite habit — one that is physically or neurologically incompatible with the old one. Running and smoking are physiologically incompatible at high intensity. Mindful eating and binge eating are neurologically incompatible. Cold water immersion and alcohol cravings are neurochemically incompatible (cold exposure floods the system with norepinephrine, which overrides the dopamine craving signal). Building the opposite habit doesn't just replace — it structurally undermines the neurological conditions that support the old one.

Chapter 06
Added Research Chapter · The Missed-Habit Protocol

The Relapse
Response

Most people who relapse don't fail because they returned to the habit once. They fail because they interpret the relapse as evidence of failure — and that interpretation ends the attempt. Research consistently shows the difference between successful and unsuccessful quitters is not whether they slipped — it's what they did the moment after they slipped.

Nevermiss twice. Once is an accident. Twice is the start of a new habit.
Slip ≠Relapse. A slip is one moment. Relapse is the interpretation that follows it.
Researchmost successful ex-smokers quit 3–5 times before quitting permanently
Shameis the primary cause of full relapse — not the slip itself (Marlatt & Gordon)
Step 01 · Immediately After
Respond, Don't React

The 5 minutes immediately after a slip are the most consequential of the entire quitting process. The brain will generate a cascade of shame, catastrophising, and "I knew I couldn't do it" narratives. These narratives are themselves the addiction talking — the habit is trying to use the slip as justification for full return. Respond deliberately: name what happened factually. "I slipped. I did the behaviour once. That's what happened."

"I slipped. I haven't quit — I had a setback. I return to the plan now."

Step 02 · Within 1 Hour
Diagnose Without Judgment

Every slip is data — extremely valuable data. Which trigger fired? Was it a time, location, emotion, person, or preceding event you hadn't mapped? What was the state of your replacement behaviour at the moment it happened? Had you slept? Eaten? Were you under unusual stress? The slip tells you exactly where your system has a gap. A diagnostic response to a slip is the fastest path to a stronger plan. A shame response produces only paralysis and full relapse.

"What exactly triggered this? What was I feeling? What can I add to my system?"

Step 03 · Same Day
Never Miss Twice — The Critical Rule

James Clear's most powerful relapse principle: never miss twice. Missing once is an accident. Missing twice is the beginning of a new habit. After a slip — no matter how bad — your singular focus is on executing the replacement behaviour before the day is over. Not tomorrow. Not "I'll start fresh on Monday." Today. The chain doesn't need to be unbroken. It needs to never have two breaks in a row.

"One slip does not define me. Two in a row would start to. I act now."

Step 04 · Ongoing
Reframe Slips as Learning Events

Research on successful long-term quitters (Prochaska's Transtheoretical Model) shows that most people who permanently change a behaviour do so after multiple attempts. Each attempt provides data. Each slip reveals a gap in the system. People who view slips as evidence they "can't quit" have a fundamental misunderstanding of how behaviour change actually works — it is almost never linear, and multiple attempts before success is the statistical norm, not the exception.

"Every attempt teaches me something. I am not failing — I am learning how to succeed at this."

Prevention
Pre-Mortems — Plan for Failure Before It Happens

One of the highest-leverage relapse prevention tools: before your quit date, spend 15 minutes writing your pre-mortem. "In 6 weeks, I've relapsed. What are the 3 most likely reasons why?" Then build specific countermeasures for each. This exercise forces you to engage with your own vulnerability honestly — which is exactly what most people avoid. The scenarios you can name in advance are the ones you can prepare for. The ones you don't name are the ones that catch you unprepared.

"What are my 3 most likely failure scenarios? What do I do if each one happens?"

Support
Accountability Changes the Neurochemistry

Research on group support for habit change (AA, Smoke-Free programmes, Weight Watchers) consistently shows that social accountability improves long-term success rates more than any individual technique. The mechanism is neurological: the social pain of letting someone down activates similar neural circuits to physical pain — making accountability a genuine biological deterrent. Tell someone. Check in weekly. The relationship changes the behaviour — not just the strategy.

"Who do I trust to check in with me about this, honestly and without judgment, once a week?"

Chapter 07
Added Research Chapter · Make the Bad Habit Hard, Good Habit Easy

Environment
Architecture

BJ Fogg's research at Stanford established that behaviour is a function of motivation, ability, and prompt — and that ability (friction) is the most modifiable. You cannot reliably out-motivate a frictionless bad habit. But you can out-architect it. Design your environment so that the habit physically cannot run on autopilot — and the replacement behaviour is the path of least resistance.

Add Friction to the Bad Habit

Every additional step between the cue and the routine reduces the probability that the habit fires.

Phone addiction: Put your phone in a drawer (not the table) when working. Delete the apps that trigger mindless use. Set a 60-second delay screen before social media opens. Each friction point forces conscious decision rather than automatic response.

Alcohol: Don't keep it in the house. The friction of a shop trip during a craving is often enough to interrupt the loop.

Smoking: Never keep cigarettes on your person. Carry them in the car, not your pocket. Distance = friction = survival time during a craving.

Late-night snacking: Don't keep trigger foods in visible locations. Put them in opaque containers at the back of cupboards. The research: people eat 3× more when food is visible vs hidden.

🛤️
Remove Friction from the Replacement

Every additional step between the trigger and the replacement behaviour reduces the probability that the replacement fires.

Exercise: Sleep in your workout clothes. Running shoes at the door, not in the wardrobe. Gym bag packed and visible. Each friction removal increases the probability that the replacement happens automatically.

Reading: Leave the book on your pillow where the phone used to be. Make it the visible option.

Healthy eating: Pre-cut vegetables at eye level in the fridge. Healthy snacks on the counter. Unhealthy ones in opaque containers at the back.

James Clear's rule: make good habits 2 steps easier and bad habits 2 steps harder. This single environmental redesign produces more behaviour change than months of willpower.

ENVIRONMENT 01
🆕
Context Change — The Nuclear Option

The most powerful environmental habit-breaking tool available: change your context entirely. Moving house, changing jobs, starting a new relationship — all have been documented to produce spontaneous, effort-free habit change at rates far exceeding deliberate attempts in the same context. The reason: habits are stored in association with specific contextual cues. Remove the context and many of the cues disappear with it. When you can't change your whole context, change your micro-context: a different route to work, a new desk position, a different morning location. New contexts give new habits room to form without competing with the old cue architecture.

Highest Leverage
ENVIRONMENT 02
Visibility Is Behaviour

Cornell food researcher Brian Wansink: people eat 3× more candy when it's in a clear bowl vs an opaque one. The same quantity. The same person. The only difference: visibility. What you see, you do. What you don't see, you don't do — at least not automatically. Apply this universally: make the replacement behaviour visible (book on pillow, running shoes at door, healthy food at eye level). Make the bad habit invisible (phone in drawer, cigarettes at a distance, trigger food hidden). Visibility shapes behaviour more reliably than intention.

Simple & Powerful
ENVIRONMENT 03
The Digital Environment

For digital habits (social media, gaming, streaming, pornography): the environment architecture is almost entirely within app settings and device configuration. Delete apps entirely (reinstalling takes more friction than the craving window). Move remaining apps off the home screen and into folders 3 layers deep. Set screen time limits with a passcode managed by someone else. Use website blockers (Cold Turkey, Freedom) with scheduled access windows. Turn off all notifications. The goal: make the digital environment work for your quit, not against it.

Digital Habits
✅ The Environment Audit — Do This on Day 1

Before your quit date, walk through your physical and digital environment and ask: "Where does this habit live — and what would happen if I removed every trace of it from this space?" Remove the alcohol from the house. Delete the apps. Put the cigarettes in the garage. Move the trigger food. Reorganise the visible objects on your desk. A clean environment doesn't guarantee success — but a contaminated environment almost guarantees failure. Every object that supports the old habit is an invitation your brain will eventually accept.
