What Your Brain
Runs On

Your brain is a high-energy organ consuming 20% of your daily calories. It is 60% fat by solid weight — and DHA omega-3 is the most critical component of that fat. What you eat doesn't just fuel your body. It builds, maintains, and repairs the physical structure of the organ generating your thoughts, emotions, and decisions. Every meal is a brain decision.

Salmon & Fatty Fish
DHA + EPA Omega-3 · Vitamin D · B12

The richest source of DHA — the omega-3 that builds brain cell membranes and supports communication between neurons. A 2022 review of 9 studies confirmed omega-3 improves learning, memory, cognitive wellbeing, and blood flow in the brain. The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of DHA counter neuroinflammation and oxidative stress directly. Aim for 2–3 servings per week. Sardines and mackerel are equally effective at a fraction of the cost.

Blueberries & Berries
Anthocyanins · Flavonoids · Vitamin C

Packed with anthocyanins — powerful flavonoids linked to improved memory and reduced cognitive decline. Research shows berry consumption improves blood flow to the brain, enhances brain plasticity (the ability to form new connections), and protects neurons from oxidative damage. Northwestern Medicine: blueberries may improve concentration, learning, and memory while protecting the brain from the aging process. Even frozen blueberries retain full potency — picked at peak ripeness and flash-frozen to lock in nutrients.

Walnuts
ALA Omega-3 · BDNF · Antioxidants

The standout brain nut. Rich in ALA (plant-based omega-3) which increases secretion of BDNF — brain-derived neurotrophic factor, the protein that supports neurogenesis, neuron survival, memory, and learning. A 2021 systematic review found a strong association between walnut consumption and improved cognitive performance. Also protective against neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's. 30g (a small handful) daily provides significant benefit.

Eggs
Choline · Tryptophan · Protein

One of the most nutrient-dense brain foods available. Choline in eggs is essential for memory and communication between brain cells — and most people are deficient. Tryptophan supports serotonin production, directly impacting mood. B vitamins support neurotransmitter production. The cholesterol concern? Largely debunked — research shows healthy adults can eat 1–2 eggs daily without negative cholesterol impact. The yolk contains almost all the brain-relevant nutrition.

Leafy Greens
Folate · Vitamin K · Lutein

Spinach, kale, Swiss chard — folate powerhouses essential for serotonin synthesis. Low folate is associated with higher depression rates (NIH). Vitamin K in leafy greens is linked to slower cognitive decline. Lutein and zeaxanthin protect neurons from oxidative damage. The research: eating leafy greens daily is one of the most consistently evidence-backed dietary behaviours for long-term brain health. The goal: at least one serving daily, any preparation method.

Dark Chocolate (70%+)
Flavanols · Magnesium · Theobromine

Dark chocolate (70%+ cocoa) contains flavanols that increase blood flow to the brain, improve neuroplasticity, and enhance mood via endorphin release. Theobromine provides a gentler, longer-lasting stimulation than caffeine. Magnesium in dark chocolate supports over 300 enzymatic reactions including those involved in serotonin synthesis. The practical rule: 20–30g of 70%+ dark chocolate daily earns the brain benefits without the sugar cost of lower-cocoa options.

The Top 10
Nutrition Myths

The nutrition space is one of the most heavily contaminated information environments available to consumers. Fad diets, wellness influencers, food industry marketing, and social media pseudoscience have created a landscape where false beliefs are more visible than the evidence. Here are 10 of the most pervasive myths — and what the research actually says.

Industry Myths
vs Research
Myth 01 · Macros
"Carbs make you fat."

Excess calories cause fat gain — regardless of their source. Low-carb diets can produce effective short-term weight loss but show no advantage over balanced diets at 12 months (DIETFITS trial, 2018: 6kg vs 5.3kg difference — statistically negligible). Complex carbohydrates from whole grains, fruits, and legumes actively support metabolic health. The problem is refined carbs and added sugar — not carbohydrates as a category.

⚡ Busted — Partially True at Best
Myth 02 · Fats
"Fat is the enemy — go low-fat."

The low-fat craze of the 1990s was one of the most damaging public nutrition mistakes in history. Fat should constitute 20–35% of daily calories and is essential for hormone production, organ insulation, and nutrient absorption. The key distinction is fat type: unsaturated fats (olive oil, avocados, nuts, fish) actively reduce cardiovascular risk. "Low-fat" products typically replace fat with sugar or sodium — making them nutritionally worse while maintaining the label.

⚡ Busted — Outdated 1990s Science
Myth 03 · Hydration
"Drink 8 glasses of water daily."

No major health authority recommends a fixed 8-glass target. The "8×8 rule" traces to a 1945 US Food & Nutrition Board report — whose important caveat that food supplies water was lost in translation. Thirst is a reliable guide for healthy adults. Coffee, tea, and other beverages count toward hydration. The old claim that caffeine dehydrates has been debunked — moderate coffee (250–400mg caffeine daily) does not cause dehydration in regular consumers.

⚡ Nuanced — Thirst Is Enough
Myth 04 · Labels
"Low-fat = healthy, fat-free = better."

Mayo Clinic is direct: low-fat and fat-free products frequently contain added sugar, sodium, or artificial thickeners to compensate for lost flavour. Fat also produces satiety — so fat-free products often result in eating more overall, counteracting any caloric benefit. Always check the full nutrition label, not just the headline claim. "Made with whole grains" and "multigrain" are similarly misleading — look for "100% whole grain" listed first in ingredients.

⚡ Busted — Industry Marketing
Myth 05 · Eggs
"Eggs raise your cholesterol — limit them."

For healthy adults, dietary cholesterol has minimal impact on blood cholesterol. The body downregulates its own cholesterol production in response to dietary intake. EUFIC 2025: "Concerns about cholesterol are mostly overstated — healthy adults can enjoy 1–2 eggs a day without raising cholesterol." Eggs are among the most complete, nutrient-dense foods available: choline, B12, high-quality protein, vitamin D, and brain-critical tryptophan.

⚡ Busted — For Healthy Adults
Myth 06 · Frozen Food
"Fresh vegetables are more nutritious than frozen."

Frozen vegetables are often MORE nutritious than fresh. "Fresh" spinach may travel for up to two weeks losing up to 50% of its Vitamin C through oxidation. Frozen produce is harvested at peak ripeness and flash-frozen within hours — locking in the full nutrient profile. They're also cheaper and reduce food waste. The practical conclusion: frozen vegetables are a nutritionally equivalent and often superior alternative to out-of-season fresh produce.

⚡ Busted — Buy Frozen Confidently
Myth 07 · Supplements
"You need multivitamins for optimal health."

Multivitamins are an "insurance policy" — not a solution. For most healthy adults eating a varied diet, they provide no measurable health benefit. Real food contains thousands of phytochemicals, fibre types, and enzymes that work synergistically — none of which can be replicated in a pill. Supplements are genuinely beneficial in specific cases (B12 for vegans, folate in pregnancy, Vitamin D for low-sunlight exposure) — but as targeted interventions, not as a dietary substitute.

⚡ Nuanced — Food First, Always
Myth 08 · Gluten
"Gluten-free is healthier for everyone."

Celiac disease affects ~1% of the population. Non-celiac gluten sensitivity affects perhaps another 6%. For the remaining 93% of people: there is no proven health benefit to avoiding gluten — and significant downsides. Gluten-free processed foods typically use tapioca starch and rice flour, producing nutritionally inferior products with higher glycaemic indexes and lower fibre content. For non-celiacs, a gluten-free diet often means eating worse — not better.

⚡ Busted — Only for Celiac/Sensitivity
Myth 09 · Seed Oils
"Seed oils are toxic and cause inflammation."

This is one of 2024–25's most viral nutrition myths. High-quality research shows eating more linoleic acid (the omega-6 in seed oils) does NOT increase inflammatory markers — and higher linoleic acid levels are associated with LOWER inflammation. The body tightly regulates fatty acid levels. A 2020 review found canola oil lowered cholesterol and improved cardiovascular risk factors. Olive oil is preferable for daily cooking, but seed oils are not the health crisis social media claims.

⚡ Busted — Social Media Panic
Myth 10 · Spot Reduction
"You can burn belly fat with specific exercises."

Spot reduction is physiologically impossible. 100 crunches per day will build abdominal muscle — but will not burn the fat layer above it. Fat loss is systemic: your body draws from its entire fat store based on genetics and hormonal patterns — not the muscles being exercised. For most people, the abdomen is the first place fat is stored and the last place it's lost. This is genetically determined. Only a sustained caloric deficit produces fat loss — from wherever your body decides first.

⚡ Busted — Physiologically Impossible

EUFIC 2025 is unequivocal: "Plant proteins can fully meet dietary needs — eating a variety ensures all essential amino acids without meat." Rice is low in lysine but high in methionine. Lentils are high in lysine but low in methionine. Together they provide all 9 essential amino acids. The classic Indian dhal-rice combination is nutritionally complete protein science in practice — developed intuitively over centuries. One of the most cost-effective complete protein meals available globally.

Greek yogurt with nuts, eggs with whole grain toast, cheese and vegetables: all complete combinations. For vegetarians who consume dairy and eggs, getting complete protein is effortless with any mixed meal.

One of the rare plant foods that is a complete protein on its own. Contains all 9 essential amino acids in adequate quantities, plus significant fibre, magnesium, iron, and B vitamins. Quinoa has a higher protein content than most grains (8g per cooked cup) and is naturally gluten-free. A versatile base for bowls, salads, and sides — the most nutritionally complete grain alternative available.

Soy-based foods are complete plant proteins and among the highest quality. Tofu provides ~8g protein per 100g. Tempeh (fermented soy) provides ~19g protein per 100g — higher than most meats by weight. Edamame (young soybeans) contains all essential amino acids and is high in fibre, vitamins, and minerals. For vegans, soy-based foods are among the easiest and most versatile complete protein sources.

Hummus + Whole Grain Pita / Vegetable Sticks

Hummus (chickpeas + tahini/sesame paste) is a clever traditional combination: chickpeas are high in lysine, sesame is high in methionine and cysteine — together they create a complete amino acid profile. Paired with whole grain pita or any grain-based vehicle, the combination is both nutritionally complete and highly palatable. The Middle Eastern tradition of hummus, pita, and vegetable combinations has been delivering complete plant protein for thousands of years.

✓ COMPLETE
PROTEIN
✅ The Amino Acid Pool — You Don't Need to Combine at Every Meal

Modern nutritional science clarified a critical misunderstanding: you do not need to combine complementary proteins at every meal. The body maintains a free amino acid pool — drawing from multiple meals throughout the day to assemble complete proteins. As long as you eat a variety of plant proteins across your daily meals, your body constructs complete proteins from the pool. The practical rule: eat a wide variety of legumes, grains, nuts, seeds, and vegetables across your day — and your amino acid needs will be met.

Meal Timing
Science

Emerging research in circadian nutrition — led by Satchin Panda at the Salk Institute — shows that when you eat is almost as important as what you eat. Your organs have their own clocks. Eating in alignment with your circadian rhythm produces measurably different metabolic outcomes than eating the same food at misaligned times.

8hrsoptimal eating window supported by circadian nutrition research
Insulinsensitivity is highest in the morning — the best time for carbohydrate-heavy meals
3hrsbefore bed — cut off food to protect sleep quality and metabolic health
Breakfastprotein at breakfast reduces appetite and caloric intake for up to 12 hours
7–9AM
Morning Window
🌅 Protein + Complex Carbs

Insulin sensitivity is highest in the morning — meaning your body processes carbohydrates most efficiently early in the day. Cortisol (the alertness hormone) is also peaking, supporting metabolism. High-protein breakfast reduces appetite hormones (ghrelin) for up to 12 hours. Practical: eggs with whole grain toast, Greek yogurt with berries, or oatmeal with nuts. This is the meal where carbohydrates do the most good with the least metabolic cost.

12–2PM
Peak Performance
💪 Largest Meal of the Day

Research consistently supports shifting caloric intake earlier rather than later. Midday is the optimal window for your largest meal: digestive enzymes are at peak activity, insulin sensitivity remains high, and your body can fully use the energy over the active afternoon. Cultures that eat their largest meal at midday (Mediterranean, Latin American) tend to have better metabolic markers than those eating largest at dinner. Include protein, healthy fats, vegetables, and complex carbs.

3–5PM
Energy Dip Window
🍌 Smart Snacking

The post-lunch energy dip (2–4PM) is real and circadian — not just a blood sugar crash. A small, nutrient-dense snack at this window maintains energy without spiking insulin. Best options: a small handful of nuts (walnuts, almonds), apple with nut butter, or dark chocolate with a piece of fruit. Avoid high-sugar snacks here — the subsequent blood sugar crash worsens the afternoon dip rather than resolving it.

6–7PM
Evening Meal
🥗 Light + Protein-Rich Dinner

Insulin sensitivity declines in the evening — meaning carbohydrates at dinner produce a higher insulin response than the same food at midday. Shifting toward protein and vegetables at dinner and reducing starchy carbohydrates produces measurably better metabolic outcomes over time. This doesn't mean no carbohydrates — it means the ratio should shift. Salmon with roasted vegetables, a lentil soup, or a protein-rich salad are ideal dinner profiles.

Stop by 8PM
Eating Cutoff
🌙 Protect Sleep & Metabolism

Eating within 2–3 hours of sleep disrupts melatonin production, raises body temperature (both impair sleep onset), and forces metabolic processing during the body's programmed repair window. Research on time-restricted eating (TRE) consistently shows that compressing eating to an 8–10 hour window produces metabolic benefits even without caloric restriction. The target: a minimum 12-hour fast between your last meal and breakfast. This is achievable without conscious caloric restriction.

Consistency
The Most Important Variable
🔄 Eat at Regular Times

Circadian nutrition research reveals that consistent meal timing — eating at roughly the same times each day — synchronises your body's clock genes and optimises metabolic function independently of what you eat. Irregular meal timing (common in modern professional life) disrupts circadian rhythms in organs including the liver, pancreas, and gut — reducing metabolic efficiency and increasing inflammation risk. The practical takeaway: consistent meal timing may be as important as the meals themselves.

Added Research Chapter · The Gut-Brain Axis · Your Second Brain

The Gut-Brain
Axis

The gut contains 500 million neurons — more than the spinal cord. 90% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gut. The bidirectional communication highway between your gut microbiome and your brain is now one of the most researched areas in neuroscience — with direct implications for mood, cognition, anxiety, and even memory.

🦠
What Your Microbiome Does

Your gut houses approximately 100 trillion microorganisms — roughly equal to the number of cells in your body. These microbes: produce neurotransmitters (serotonin, GABA, dopamine), regulate immune function (80% of immune cells live in the gut), synthesise vitamins (B12, K2), metabolise nutrients from food, and communicate directly with the brain via the vagus nerve.

The gut-brain axis is the bidirectional communication between your enteric nervous system (the gut's neural network) and your central nervous system. This axis means that gut inflammation produces brain inflammation, gut health affects mood directly, and stress produces gut dysfunction simultaneously.

A 2021 meta-analysis found that probiotic supplementation measurably reduced depression and anxiety scores — establishing a direct causal link between microbiome health and mental health outcomes.

🥦
How to Feed Your Microbiome

Fibre is the foundation. Gut bacteria ferment fibre into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — particularly butyrate — which reduce inflammation, strengthen the gut lining, and support brain health. Most people get 15–17g of fibre daily; research suggests 25–38g is optimal. Increase gradually to avoid discomfort.

Diversity is the goal. The most robust research finding on microbiome health: dietary diversity produces microbiome diversity. Aim for 30+ different plant foods per week — not just 30 servings, but 30 different species. Each plant species feeds different bacterial strains.

Fermented foods are natural probiotics. Kimchi, kefir, yogurt, sauerkraut, miso, and tempeh introduce beneficial live bacteria directly. A 2021 Stanford study found that a high-fermented food diet increased microbiome diversity faster than a high-fibre diet alone.

GUT PRINCIPLE 01
The 30-Plant-Per-Week Target

The largest gut microbiome study to date (American Gut Project) found that people who ate 30+ different plant species per week had significantly more diverse microbiomes than those eating under 10. "Plants" includes vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, herbs, and spices. One teaspoon of turmeric counts. A tablespoon of mixed seeds counts. The bar is achievable — the diversity is what matters. Track your weekly plant variety for one month and watch your gut health, energy, and mood shift noticeably.

Research-Backed Target
GUT PRINCIPLE 02
🍶
Fermented Foods Daily

Including one serving of naturally fermented food daily is one of the highest-leverage dietary habits available for gut health. Options: plain kefir (30g protein and billions of live bacteria), Greek yogurt with live cultures, kimchi or sauerkraut as a condiment, miso soup, or kombucha. The key word is "naturally fermented" — commercially produced pickles in vinegar are not fermented and contain no live bacteria. The Stanford study: 10 weeks of high-fermented food diet increased microbiome diversity and reduced inflammatory markers more than high-fibre diet alone.

Daily Habit
GUT PRINCIPLE 03
What Harms the Microbiome

Ultra-processed food: Emulsifiers (carboxymethylcellulose, polysorbate 80) found in ultra-processed foods disrupt gut lining integrity and alter microbiome composition within days.

Unnecessary antibiotics: A single course of broad-spectrum antibiotics can reduce microbiome diversity by 25–50%, with some bacterial populations taking 6–12 months to recover.

Chronic stress: The gut-brain axis runs both ways — chronic psychological stress alters microbiome composition measurably, increasing inflammation and gut permeability.

Protect the Microbiome

Added Research Chapter · The Root Cause Protocol

Anti-Inflammatory
Eating

Chronic low-grade inflammation is now identified as a root cause or major contributor to heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, Alzheimer's, cancer, and arthritis. Diet is the most modifiable driver of systemic inflammation. The anti-inflammatory diet is not a specific protocol — it is a set of principles that consistently reduces inflammatory markers across all populations studied.

Pro-Inflammatory Foods — Increase Markers

Ultra-processed foods: The strongest dietary driver of inflammation across all research. Refined carbohydrates, artificial additives, trans fats, and seed oils used in commercial frying all elevate CRP (C-reactive protein) — the primary inflammatory marker.

Added sugar: Drives inflammation through insulin spikes, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), and gut microbiome disruption. The average person consumes 20 teaspoons of added sugar daily — 4× the recommended limit.

Refined vegetable oils used at high heat: Omega-6 heavy oils (corn, soybean, vegetable oil blends) used for deep-frying produce harmful oxidation products. The issue is cooking method and excessive omega-6:omega-3 ratio — not moderate seed oil consumption itself.

❄️
Anti-Inflammatory Foods — Reduce Markers

Extra virgin olive oil: Contains oleocanthal — a compound with similar anti-inflammatory mechanism to ibuprofen. The Mediterranean diet's primary fat source and a consistent predictor of cardiovascular and cognitive health in research.

Fatty fish (omega-3s): EPA and DHA directly reduce inflammatory eicosanoids and cytokines — the body's primary inflammatory signalling molecules.

Turmeric + black pepper: Curcumin in turmeric is a potent anti-inflammatory compound. Bioavailability increases 2,000% when consumed with piperine (black pepper). The combination rivals anti-inflammatory medications in some research — without the side effects.

All colourful produce: The phytochemical pigments that produce colour in fruits and vegetables (anthocyanins, lycopene, beta-carotene, quercetin) are almost universally anti-inflammatory antioxidants.

ANTI-INFLAM 01
🫒
The Mediterranean Diet — Most Researched Anti-Inflammatory Pattern

The Mediterranean diet is the most extensively studied dietary pattern in human research — and the most consistently associated with reduced inflammation, lower cardiovascular risk, lower Alzheimer's risk, and higher longevity. Its key elements: abundant olive oil, fish (2–3× weekly), legumes (daily), whole grains, vegetables and fruits (abundant), nuts, moderate dairy, minimal red meat and processed food. No food is forbidden — the pattern, not the individual foods, produces the protective effect. This is not a restriction diet. It is an abundance diet.

Best-Evidenced Pattern
ANTI-INFLAM 02
🌶️
The Power Spices

Turmeric: Curcumin inhibits NF-κB — the primary switch for inflammation at the cellular level. Add to everything. Requires black pepper for absorption.

Ginger: Gingerols and shogaols have documented anti-inflammatory effects equivalent to some NSAIDs in joint pain research.

Garlic: Allicin activates anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways. Most effective consumed raw or lightly cooked — high heat destroys allicin. Let crushed garlic sit 10 minutes before cooking to stabilise the compound.

Cinnamon: Reduces fasting blood glucose and insulin response — indirectly reducing inflammation from chronic blood sugar elevation.

Add to Every Meal
ANTI-INFLAM 03
The Omega-3:Omega-6 Ratio

The optimal omega-3 to omega-6 ratio for anti-inflammatory effect is approximately 1:4. The modern Western diet averages 1:20–1:40 — producing a chronic pro-inflammatory state. The solution is twofold: increase omega-3 intake (fatty fish, walnuts, flaxseed, chia seeds) and reduce omega-6 from ultra-processed and commercially fried food (the largest source). You don't need to eliminate omega-6 — you need to rebalance the ratio. Two portions of fatty fish per week, a daily tablespoon of flaxseed, and reduced processed food will move the ratio measurably within weeks.

Ratio Matters

Added Research Chapter · The Most Underprescribed Performance Tool

Hydration
Science

Water is the most abundant compound in the human body (60% of body weight) and the most undervalued performance intervention available. A 2% reduction in hydration status produces measurable cognitive impairment, mood deterioration, and reduced physical performance. And most people are chronically mildly dehydrated without knowing it.

HYDRATION 01
The 2% Rule — When Dehydration Becomes Cognitive Impairment

Research consistently shows that a 2% reduction in body water produces: reduced working memory, impaired attention and concentration, increased perception of task difficulty, elevated cortisol and mood deterioration, and reduced physical performance in endurance tasks. By the time you feel thirsty, you are already at approximately 1–2% dehydration. The practical implication: thirst is not an early warning system — it is a delayed alarm. For cognitive performance in particular, maintaining hydration throughout the day (not just catching up when thirsty) is the strategy.

🔬 Brain Impact

The brain is 75% water. Even mild dehydration causes brain tissue to contract — which activates pain receptors inside the skull. The classic "dehydration headache" is the brain physically shrinking. Maintaining hydration keeps the brain at optimal volume, optimal neurotransmitter production, and optimal electrical signal conductivity.

Performance Critical
HYDRATION 02
☕
Coffee, Tea & Other Beverages Count

The myth that caffeine dehydrates has been comprehensively debunked. Moderate coffee consumption (250–400mg caffeine daily, approximately 3–4 cups) does not cause dehydration in habitual coffee drinkers. The mild diuretic effect of caffeine is outweighed by the fluid volume of the beverage. Tea, herbal drinks, milk, and even moderate fruit juice all contribute to daily fluid intake. The practical guidance: drink a variety of beverages throughout the day and trust thirst as your primary guide — but don't wait until thirst to start your fluid intake.

Myth Busted
HYDRATION 03
Optimal Hydration Protocol for Performance

Morning: 500ml of water immediately after waking — you've been 7–8 hours without fluid and lost water through breathing. This is the highest-impact hydration habit available.

Before meals: 250–500ml of water 30 minutes before eating supports digestion and can reduce caloric intake by 13–22% (preloading research).

Colour check: Urine colour is the most reliable and accessible hydration marker. Pale straw yellow = optimal. Dark yellow = dehydrated. Clear = overhydrated (rare but possible with excessive water forcing).

Electrolytes: For intense exercise or hot climates, add sodium, potassium, and magnesium — water alone without electrolytes can cause hyponatremia in endurance contexts.
